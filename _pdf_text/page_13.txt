These findings suggest that controlling only the aggregate
popularity proportion is insufficient; position-aware exposure
regulation emerges as a critical requirement for achieving
robust rank-level calibration. In this sense, SUPER can be
interpreted as a rank-aware popularity calibration frame-
work, where the user-level head–tail ratio constrains overall
composition while the blueprint-based merge regularizes the
positional structure of exposure.
Several factors contribute to this superior calibration
behavior. First, SUPER treats user popularity inclination as
a dynamic reference rather than a rigid global constraint,
mitigating the risk of overcorrection commonly observed
in optimization-heavy methods. Second, its effectiveness
across diverse backbone recommenders (HPF, NeuMF,
SKMeans, VaeCF, and WBPR) demonstrates that the calibra-
tion gains are not architecture-dependent. Finally, its stable
performance in sparse domains highlights the advantage
of lightweight, user-aligned exposure control over more
assumption-intensive strategies.
Overall, the calibration results demonstrate that SUPER
more effectively captures the interplay between mainstream
and niche interests at both the global and rank levels com-
pared to re-ranking-based, causal, representation-driven, and
robustness-oriented debiasing methods. By jointly minimiz-
ing Rmse-PC and MRMC, SUPER delivers recommendation
lists that remain closely aligned with users’ interaction-
derived popularity preferences throughout the entire top-N
sequence.
B. BEYOND-ACCURACY DIMENSIONS: APLT, ENTROPY,
LTC, AND NOVELTY
While effective calibration is vital for user satisfaction,
recommender systems also benefit from promoting diversity,
facilitating item discovery, and broadening coverage of
less popular content. We therefore assess APLT (Average
Percentage of Long-Tail Items), Entropy, LTC (Long-Tail
Coverage), and Novelty to capture multiple beyond-accuracy
dimensions. These metrics provide complementary insights
into how different debiasing strategies, including re-ranking-
based, causality-aware, and representation-driven methods,
affect exposure patterns beyond pure relevance.
1) APLT
A higher APLT reflects the extent to which a debiasing
method promotes long-tail items within the top-N recom-
mendations, thereby encouraging exposure to less popular
content. As illustrated in Figure 4, methods such as MUL
and representation-oriented approaches including DICE and
CausE achieve elevated APLT scores by aggressively priori-
tizing underrepresented items. While this strategy increases
tail exposure, it may also introduce deviations from users’
historical popularity tendencies, a pattern consistent with
their higher calibration errors observed in Rmse-PC and
MRMC.
In contrast, recent optimization-driven frameworks such as
PDRO and PPAC exhibit markedly low APLT values across
backbone recommenders, indicating limited integration of
tail items into the recommendation lists. This behavior
suggests that distributional robustness or adaptive calibration
objectives alone do not necessarily translate into effective
long-tail promotion, and may instead preserve head-dominant
recommendation structures.
Across all settings, SUPER achieves a balanced incorpo-
ration of tail items, maintaining competitive APLT without
indiscriminately saturating recommendation lists with niche
content. Rather than enforcing uniform corrective shifts,
SUPER regulates tail exposure in accordance with each user’s
popularity inclination, enabling meaningful discovery while
preserving behavioral coherence.
