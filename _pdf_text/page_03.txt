III. THE PROPOSED SMART USER-CENTRIC POPULARITY
DEBIASING METHOD
One of the main objectives in tackling popularity bias is to
ensure that recommendation lists reflect each user’s observed
preferences regarding item popularity. While many existing
techniques simply adjust item rankings post-hoc or rely on
global debiasing strategies, our approach provides a more
personalized and principled solution. By integrating the
Pareto principle with user-specific popularity inclinations,
we create a smart recommendation framework that harmo-
nizes both popular and less-known (tail) items within the final
recommendations.
SUPER is not intended as a causal debiasing approach;
rather, it is a calibration-oriented framework designed
for standard offline recommendation settings where
impression/exposure logs are unavailable. Accordingly,
SUPER focuses on controllable exposure calibration under
interaction-only benchmarks, where structurally separating
head and tail interactions provides a practical way to reduce
representation imbalance prior to ranking-time calibration.

A. PRE-PROCESSING: SEPARATING THE DATASET INTO
POPULAR AND TAIL ITEMS
We begin by applying the Pareto principle to partition the
entire item catalog into two distinct subsets:
1) Popular (Head) Items (H): Items are sorted in
descending order according to their rating frequency,
and the ‘‘popular’’ set is defined as the minimal
prefix of this sorted list whose cumulative rating count
reaches 20% of all ratings in the dataset. This means
that popular items are those accounting for 20% of the
total interaction volume, rather than the top 20% of
items by count, capturing the mainstream portion of the
catalog that receives disproportionately high attention
2) Tail Items (T): The remaining 80% of the catalog,
items that have not attained widespread consumption,
form the long-tail set. These items, though less
frequently interacted with, can be crucial for delivering
diverse and personalized recommendations to users
with unique tastes.
This preprocessing step clearly distinguishes between
mainstream (popular) and niche (tail) content. By isolating
the two sets upfront, we reduce the risk that the dominance of
popular items overshadows the learning of tail-item signals
in models specialized for niche content.
Although the head–tail partitioning may appear
structurally simple, it serves a principled role in miti-
gating representation imbalance during model learning.
In popularity-skewed datasets, jointly training on mixed
interactions often biases latent representations toward head
items, suppressing informative signals from the long-
tail. By separating these subsets, the proposed framework
introduces an implicit exposure regularization effect that
stabilizes learning dynamics prior to calibration. The
objective of this design is therefore not increased architectural
complexity, but controlled representation learning that
preserves minority-item signals while remaining compatible
with diverse backbone recommenders.
B. TRAINING ON POPULAR AND TAIL SUBSETS
SEPARATELY
After dividing the dataset, we train distinct recommendation
models, one using only the popular subset and another using
only the tail subset. This approach allows each model to
specialize and learn latent patterns more effectively without
being biased by the other subset’s distribution. For example:
• Popular-Only Model: Trained on the H set, it excels at
recommending widely-liked items.
• Tail-Only Model: Trained on the T set, it focuses on
uncovering hidden gems and niche content that might
otherwise be overshadowed by popular items.
Training these models independently decouples the learn-
ing processes, ensuring that the tail-specialized model
can gain a robust understanding of lesser-known con-
tent without the interference of highly rated mainstream
items. This decoupled training is intended to prevent
popularity-dominant gradients from leaking into tail rep-
resentations, which can occur under a shared embedding
space. In this sense, the split-and-train design functions as a
structural constraint that improves controllability of head–tail
exposure before the final user-level calibration step.
Algorithm 1 summarizes this pre-processing stage by
(i) applying Pareto-based head–tail partitioning and
(ii) training separate models on the popular and tail subsets.
We note that the popular-item and tail-item components
in SUPER are intentionally trained independently. While
cross-subset knowledge transfer or shared representations
could potentially improve raw tail-item accuracy, such
mechanisms may also reintroduce popularity signals into
tail representations and thereby weaken exposure con-
trol. Following common practice in popularity-bias miti-
gation, SUPER prioritizes controlled popularity exposure
through decoupled modeling and post-processing calibra-
tion, rather than joint representation learning. Exploring
cross-subset transfer mechanisms is therefore treated as
a complementary research direction and left for future
work.
