C. RANKING ACCURACY: nDCG
Although the preceding analyses emphasize calibration and
beyond-accuracy dimensions, ranking accuracy remains a
fundamental determinant of recommendation quality. The
nDCG (normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain) metric
evaluates how effectively each method positions relevant
items near the top of the ranked list. Figure 8 presents
results for the HPF, NeuMF, SKMeans, VaeCF, and WBPR
recommenders under a diverse set of popularity-debiasing
strategies.
Several methods achieve strong ranking accuracy by prior-
itizing relevance preservation during debiasing. In particular,
CausalEPP consistently delivers the highest nDCG scores,
while calibration-oriented approaches such as PPAC also
demonstrate competitive ranking performance. These results
suggest that exposure-aware correction and personalized
calibration can effectively mitigate popularity bias without
severely disrupting relevance ordering.
Importantly, SUPER remains highly competitive in terms
of nDCG across datasets and backbone recommenders,
despite simultaneously optimizing for calibration and
beyond-accuracy objectives. Unlike methods that prioritize
ranking accuracy through stronger exposure corrections,
SUPER adopts a balanced strategy that regulates head–tail
composition at the user level while preserving stable
relevance signals.
This behavior highlights a key insight: maximizing ranking
accuracy alone does not necessarily guarantee user-aligned
recommendation quality. Methods that achieve the high-
est nDCG may still exhibit weaker calibration or less
controlled exposure patterns (see Sections V-A and V-B).
By contrast, SUPER demonstrates that strong ranking perfor-
mance can coexist with improved popularity alignment and
diversity.
Furthermore, the stability of SUPER’s nDCG across
diverse base recommenders indicates that its benefits are not
architecture-dependent. Even in challenging recommenda-
tion environments, the method maintains reliable relevance
ordering while mitigating popularity bias. Collectively, these
findings suggest that user-centric popularity calibration and
accurate ranking are complementary rather than conflicting
objectives.
These findings reinforce the central premise of this work:
effective popularity debiasing should be evaluated not solely
through ranking accuracy, but through its ability to balance
relevance, calibration, and exposure.
