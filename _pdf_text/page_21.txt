3) DYNAMICS OF POPULARITY AND USER PREFERENCE
DRIFT
The current formulation of SUPER estimates item popularity
and user inclination under a static offline setting. However,
the framework naturally supports dynamic updates with
minimal modification. Item popularity statistics can be
recomputed periodically or within sliding temporal win-
dows to reflect evolving consumption trends. Similarly, the
user-specific popularity inclination Popu can be updated
incrementally using recent interactions, enabling the system
to adapt when a user’s preference shifts toward more
popular or more niche content over time. Importantly,
such updates do not require retraining the underlying
recommendation models; only the lightweight popularity
statistics and post-processing ratios need to be refreshed.
This design makes SUPER compatible with drift-aware and
real-time recommendation pipelines, although a full temporal
evaluation is left for future work.
B. DATA, SCALABILITY, AND DEPLOYMENT CONSTRAINTS
Another limitation arises from the system’s data require-
ments. By design, SUPER depends on meaningful historical
user interactions to gauge how strongly individuals lean
toward popular or less-known items. This requirement poses
challenges in cold-start conditions where new users or newly
introduced products lack sufficient interaction logs. Under
these constraints, calibrating head–tail preferences becomes
less reliable, and the model may offer suboptimal suggestions
until enough data accumulates.
Further considerations pertain to the computational over-
head associated with integrating user-centric popularity
estimation into each recommendation. While the hybrid
pre- and post-processing approach in SUPER is generally
feasible for moderate data scales, very large systems with
millions of active users and items might demand further
optimization. Techniques such as incremental updates to
the popularity classification or parallelized operations could
mitigate performance bottlenecks, but the extent of these
modifications remains to be assessed.
Additionally, privacy-oriented constraints can limit the
availability of detailed user interaction data. As SUPER relies
on clear signals of user preferences and item popularity,
approaches that blur or anonymize these inputs may reduce its
precision in distinguishing head and tail items or in estimating
a user’s observed inclination for mainstream versus niche
content.
C. ASSUMPTION SENSITIVITY AND POTENTIAL NEGATIVE
EFFECTS
1) SENSITIVITY TO THE PARETO THRESHOLD
Although the 20% head threshold provides stable perfor-
mance across datasets, the definition of popularity directly
influences the estimation of user inclination and the resulting
exposure calibration. Lower thresholds impose a stricter
notion of popularity and may improve calibration, whereas
higher thresholds can blur the distinction between head
and tail items, potentially weakening exposure control. This
sensitivity highlights that popularity partitioning is not purely
structural but also assumption-dependent.
2) FAILURE MODES OF THE BLUEPRINT STRATEGY
The blueprint-based merge uses a user’s historical head–tail
pattern as a soft positional prior for interleaving popular
and tail candidates. For users with sparse, short, or noisy
histories, this pattern may be unreliable and can inject
incidental exposure noise into rank-level ordering. Notably,
SUPER’s main calibration behavior is governed by the
hard head–tail quota derived from Popu, while the blueprint
only affects within-list placement as long as quota remains.
Therefore, in cold-start or low-activity regimes, a robust
variant can downweight or disable the blueprint and rely on
proportional (quota-preserving) interleaving, optionally with
simple smoothing based on history length.
3) REPRESENTATION FRAGMENTATION FROM
INDEPENDENT TRAINING
Training separate models on head and tail subsets improves
exposure controllability but may reduce cross-subset knowl-
edge transfer. Consequently, latent signals learned in the pop-
ular domain may not fully inform tail-item representations,
potentially limiting recommendation quality for niche items.
Joint or partially shared representations could mitigate this
effect but may simultaneously reintroduce popularity bias.
4) PERSONALIZATION-DIVERSITY TRADE-OFF
While SUPER aims to calibrate exposure rather than maxi-
mize diversity, increasing tail representation may not benefit
all users equally. Popularity-oriented users may experience
slight relevance degradation when additional tail items are
introduced, whereas tail-oriented users typically benefit from
improved discovery. This reflects a fundamental trade-off
in popularity debiasing between exposure fairness and strict
personalization.
