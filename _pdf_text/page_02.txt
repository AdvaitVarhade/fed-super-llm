II. RELATED WORK
Popularity-bias mitigation strategies in recommender
systems are commonly categorized into pre-processing,
in-processing, and post-processingapproaches. Pre-processing
methods aim to reduce imbalance in the training data
before model learning, for example by separating head and
tail items or reweighting interactions to amplify under-
represented content [28], [29]. In-processing techniques
modify the learning objective or model constraints to
penalize popularity-correlated signals or explicitly optimize
for diversity and fairness [10], [11], [19], [30], [31]. While
effective, these approaches are often tightly coupled to
specific model architectures and may require substantial
redesign to generalize across recommenders.
Post-processing methods operate after a base recom-
mender generates prediction scores and adjust the final
ranked list to control exposure. Representative exam-
ples include inverse-popularity calibration [32], [33],
diversification mechanisms such as xQuAD [20], and
exposure-balancing formulations including Discrepancy
Minimization [34] and FA*IR [35]. Due to their model-
agnostic nature, post-processing approaches are particularly
attractive for benchmarking and comparative evaluation
across multiple backbone recommenders.
Among user-centric mitigation approaches, Calibrated
Popularity [21] is closely related to our work, as it seeks
to align the popularity distribution of recommendations
with a user’s historical consumption. However, a central
challenge in such calibration lies in the fact that historical
interactions may conflate preference with exposure. Treating
all consumed items equally can overestimate affinity for
popular content that was merely visible or promoted.
Some other studies therefore emphasize the need for
behaviorally meaningful user profiling that better reflects
genuine preference signals [7], [23], [24], [25]. Nevertheless,
there remains a practical gap for methods that (i) operate
consistently across diverse recommendation algorithms and
(ii) calibrate popularity exposure at the individual user
level while preserving ranking quality and beyond-accuracy
objectives.
Recent research has increasingly focused on user-aware
and optimization-driven strategies for mitigating popularity
bias. Approaches such as Popularity-aware Distributionally
Robust Optimization (PDRO) [36], Personalized Popularity-
Aware Calibration (PPAC) [37], and Causal Exposure-based
Popularity Penalization (CausalEPP) [38] exemplify this
trend by incorporating robustness, personalization, or causal
reasoning into the debiasing process. While these methods
improve exposure control through distributional optimization
or adaptive calibration, they often rely on stronger modeling
assumptions and tighter integration with the learning objec-
tive. Consequently, ensuring consistent rank-level alignment
with user-specific popularity tendencies while preserving
model-agnostic applicability remains an open challenge.
Beyond optimization-based and calibration-driven meth-
ods [37], very recent studies increasingly explore causality-
aware, robustness-oriented, and representation-level debias-
ing strategies. For example, causal graph diffusion models
have been proposed to improve out-of-distribution recom-
mendation by capturing structural dependencies beyond
observational interactions [39]. Motivation-aware retrieval
approaches further emphasize aligning long-tail exposure
with users’ latent intent rather than relying solely on
interaction frequency [40]. Similarly, causal intervention
frameworks address the evolving nature of person popularity,
highlighting the temporal dynamics of exposure bias [38].
In parallel, unbiased learning-to-rank methods based on
conditional mutual information minimization seek to disen-
tangle relevance from spurious popularity correlations [41],
while adversarial graph-dropout techniques improve robust-
ness in graph-based collaborative filtering [42].
Recent work also demonstrates that popularity bias persists
even in large language model-based recommenders, suggest-
ing that exposure imbalance remains a fundamental challenge
across emerging recommendation paradigms [43]. Tem-
poral recommenders, including the Netflix Prize-winning
approaches, demonstrate that user preferences evolve over
time; however, these models primarily optimize predictive
accuracy rather than user-level exposure calibration, leaving
popularity alignment as a complementary challenge.
Collectively, these studies, including temporal, causal,
and robustness-oriented approaches, indicate a broader shift
toward integrating personalization-aware mechanisms into
popularity-debiasing pipelines. SUPER aligns with this
emerging direction by emphasizing user-level exposure
calibration while maintaining model-agnostic applicability
under standard offline evaluation settings.
To address this gap, we propose SUPER, which combines
Pareto-based user-centric popularity profiling with a hybrid
pre- and post-processing framework. This design enables
fine-grained control over head–tail exposure at the individual
level while remaining compatible with multiple backbone
recommenders and standard offline evaluation protocols.
Rather than competing within a single methodological
paradigm, SUPER is designed to complement existing
debiasing strategies by introducing a lightweight user-centric
calibration layer that operates independently of the underly-
ing recommender. In this sense, the framework can be inter-
preted as a practical bridge between classical model-agnostic
exposure control and emerging user-aware optimization
