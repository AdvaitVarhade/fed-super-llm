Additional Remarks:
The popularity inclination proxy (Pop u) in SUPER is
computed from the user’s full interaction history to capture
overall exposure tendencies toward popular versus tail
items. Evaluation metrics such as RMSE-PC, on the other
hand, focus on positively perceived items. This distinction
allows the framework to model exposure calibration while
preserving preference-oriented evaluation.
The blueprint used in the merging stage is derived from
preference-ranked interactions rather than chronological
order. This design aims to provide a stable representation of
user preference structure, especially in public datasets where
timestamp information may be incomplete or inconsistent.
Finally, the GKPI indicator is intended as a compact
comparative summary of multiple evaluation dimensions
within the same experimental setting. Individual metrics
remain the primary basis for interpretation, while GKPI
highlights the overall balance between accuracy and long-tail
exposure.
J. KEY OBSERVATIONS AND IMPLICATIONS
The experimental findings collectively emphasize that
popularity debiasing is most effective when treated as
a user-centric calibration problem rather than a purely
system-level exposure optimization task. Instead of enforcing
uniform head–tail ratios or aggressive long-tail promotion
across all users, SUPER demonstrates that aligning recom-
mendation exposure with individual popularity inclinations
leads to a more stable and interpretable trade-off among
accuracy, diversity, and fairness objectives.
A key implication of these results is that strong calibration
does not necessarily conflict with beyond-accuracy goals.
By dynamically adjusting the head–tail composition during
list construction, SUPER achieves meaningful improvements
in long-tail exposure, diversity, and novelty while preserving
ranking quality and avoiding overcorrection. This behavior
contrasts with several competing debiasing strategies that
optimize a single objective at the expense of user-level
coherence.
From a practical perspective, these observations sug-
gest that future popularity-debiasing approaches should
incorporate personalization as a first-class component rather
than relying on global constraints or static re-ranking rules.
SUPER provides a scalable example of how user-specific
preference modeling can be integrated into post-processing
pipelines without incurring substantial computational
overhead.
Overall, the results indicate that user-aligned popularity
calibration offers a principled and robust pathway toward
fairer and more diverse recommender systems, particularly
in real-world settings characterized by heterogeneous user
preferences and data sparsity.
VI. LIMITATIONS
Although SUPER demonstrates promising effectiveness in
mitigating popularity bias and maintaining robust calibration
under controlled experimental settings, several limitations
emerge when one considers its applicability in more complex,
real-world scenarios.
A. LIMITATIONS RELATED TO POPULARITY MODELING
1) POPULARITY INCLINATION AS A SINGLE PREFERENCE
DIMENSION
We acknowledge that the popularity inclination ratio (Pop u)
captures only one dimension of user preference and does
not aim to represent the full spectrum of individual tastes.
In SUPER, popularity inclination is intentionally modeled as
an orthogonal control signal that regulates exposure to head
versus tail items, while all other preference dimensions (e.g.,
genre affinity, topical relevance, or rating intensity) remain
fully governed by the underlying backbone recommender.
In this sense, SUPER does not replace or override standard
preference modeling, but complements it by calibrating
popularity exposure at the user level.
Furthermore, since the public benchmark datasets used
in this study do not provide impression or exposure logs,
it is not possible to fully disentangle preference from
exposure in a causal sense. SUPER therefore treats popularity
inclination as a robust behavioral estimate rather than a
true latent preference. We further emphasize that popularity
debiasing inherently involves normative design choices
regarding how exposure should be regulated. SUPER makes
these assumptions explicit rather than implicit by framing
popularity inclination as an operational calibration signal
rather than a definitive representation of true user preference.

inference framework and does not attempt to recover users’
latent ‘‘true preferences’’ from observational interaction
data. Disentangling preference from exposure typically
requires impression logs, randomized interventions, or strong
identification assumptions that are not available in standard
offline recommendation benchmarks. Accordingly, SUPER
