From an integrated perspective, the compared methods
exhibit distinct strengths across evaluation dimensions.
Diversification-oriented strategies such as MUL and xQuad
tend to improve catalog coverage and novelty-related indica-
tors, whereas representation- and causality-driven approaches
including CausE, DICE, and MACR emphasize exposure con-
trol and structural bias mitigation. User-aware optimization
frameworks such as PPAC and robustness-oriented methods
like PDRO further enhance calibration-oriented or stability-
related metrics. These heterogeneous performance profiles
highlight the inherently multi-objective nature of popularity
debiasing.
In contrast, SUPER consistently achieves the highest GKPI
across datasets and backbone recommenders, indicating a
more balanced trade-off among competing recommendation
objectives. Following our evaluation protocol, comparisons
are reported against the second-best debiasing method (i.e.,
the strongest non-SUPER baseline under the seed-averaged
mean for each setting). For example, on the ML dataset
with the VaeCF recommender, SUPER attains a GKPI of
0.472, surpassing the runner-up method (PPAC, 0.321) by an
absolute margin of 0.151.
These findings suggest that regulating head–tail exposure
at the user level provides structural benefits that extend
beyond isolated metric improvements. Rather than optimiz-
ing a single dimension at the expense of others, SUPER
maintains stable performance across accuracy, calibration,
and beyond-accuracy criteria, which is particularly desirable
in real-world recommendation environments where multiple
quality dimensions must be satisfied simultaneously. This
behavior supports the interpretation of GKPI as a prac-
tical rubric for identifying methods that achieve coherent
trade-offs rather than extreme optimization of isolated
metrics.
E. RUNTIME ANALYSIS: TOP-N RECOMMENDATION
GENERATION COST
Beyond recommendation quality and calibration, compu-
tational efficiency is a critical factor for the practical
deployment of popularity-debiasing methods. We therefore
analyze the average wall-clock time required to generate top-
10 recommendation lists under different debiasing strategies.
The reported values correspond to the total post-processing
cost, including candidate selection and list construction, aver-
aged across all backbone recommenders and random seeds.
For each dataset and method, we report the mean wall-clock
time aggregated over all backbone recommenders and seeds
(i.e., averaging the per-(recommender, seed) runtimes).
All experiments were conducted on a single machine
equipped with an Apple M2 Max processor (12-core CPU,
38-core GPU), 32 GB RAM, and 1 TB SSD storage.
No additional model retraining was performed during top-
N generation; all methods operate purely at the inference
or post-processing stage. The reported times measure only
inference-time ranking and post-processing during top-N
generation. They exclude all training costs; for SUPER, the
two backbone models (head-only and tail-only) are trained
offline with the same architecture and hyperparameters, and
runtime reflects only the final merging/calibration step.
Table 3 highlights substantial differences in computational
cost among debiasing strategies. Network-flow-based meth-
ods such as Discrepancy Minimization (DM) incur the high-
est runtime overhead, reflecting their inherent optimization
complexity. Similarly, diversification-oriented approaches
such as VarERPs (which subsume AUG, MUL, and VaR) and
xQuad require repeated score recomputation and iterative re-
ranking, leading to significantly higher execution times.
