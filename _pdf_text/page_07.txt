For each user, the backbone(s) produce predicted scores over
candidate items. In the worst case, scoring and sorting over all
items incurs O(n) and O(n log n) time, respectively. SUPER
itself does not add an additional global optimization step;
after obtaining the per-pool ranked lists, it truncates them to
Cpop and Ctail of sizes Npop and Ntail with Npop + Ntail = N,
and performs the hard-quota / soft-blueprint merge in O(N)
time. Therefore, the additional overhead of SUPER beyond
backbone scoring is O(N) per user, and overall runtime is
dominated by backbone inference and (when applicable)
large-scale sorting (Table 3).
E. BENEFITS OF THE SMART USER-CENTRIC APPROACH
This method addresses several limitations of prior techniques:
User-Centric Calibration: By determining how many
recommendations should come from the popular set versus
the tail set for each individual user, we ensure that rec-
ommendations align more closely with that user’s observed
consumption distribution.
Fair Representation of the Long-Tail: Our approach
naturally incorporates tail items into the recommendation
process, promoting diversity and providing opportunities for
lesser-known items to surface.
Mitigating Popularity Bias: Rather than merely
down-ranking popular items or imposing blanket constraints,
this solution trains models separately on both subsets and then
tailors the blend of final recommendations. The result is a
more nuanced debiasing that accounts for each user’s own
level of comfort with mainstream or niche items.
Scalable and Adaptable: The approach is flexible and
can be applied to various datasets, domains, and underlying
recommendation algorithms. The pre-processing does not
assume a specific model architecture, making the framework
portable and easily integrated into different scenarios.
More broadly, SUPER can be viewed as a user-adaptive
exposure control framework that operates at the ranking layer.
Instead of relying solely on global exposure constraints or
stochastic reallocation, the method introduces a structured
yet lightweight regularization signal that preserves alignment
between observed behavioral patterns and recommendation
exposure. This perspective positions SUPER within a grow-
ing class of calibration-oriented recommender approaches
that emphasize exposure coherence alongside ranking
accuracy.
F. ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO
To clarify how the SUPER framework operates in practice,
consider a hypothetical user whose historical records indicate
a moderate preference for popular (head) items relative to
niche (tail) ones. Suppose, for instance, that this user has
interacted with 200 items in total, with 130 of those classified
as popular and the remaining 70 belonging to the tail.
From these statistics, one infers that the user’s popularity
inclination ratio is
Popu = 130
200 = 0.65.
Hence, approximately 65% of this individual’s consumed
items are from the head portion of the catalog, while 35%
are from the tail.
1) PRE-PROCESSING AND PREDICTION
Following the SUPER design, two specialized models are
trained: one (Mpop) exclusively on the head-dominant subset
and another (M tail) exclusively on the tail-dominant subset.
At recommendation time, each model produces a ranked list
of items for the user (for instance, top-10 suggestions per
model):
• Popular-Only Model Output: A descending list of items
predominantly aligned with the head part of the catalog.
• Tail-Only Model Output: A descending list emphasizing
less popular or niche items.
2) DETERMINING USER-SPECIFIC PROPORTIONS
Since Popu = 0.65, if the system is to produce a top-10
recommendation list, it will compute
Npop = ⌊10× 0.65⌋ =6, Ntail = 10 − 6 = 4.
This calculation instructs SUPER to select 6 items from the
user-specific popular-only ranking and 4 items from the tail-
only ranking.
3) CONSTRUCTING THE SMART RECOMMENDATION LIST
While each model’s output (M pop and Mtail) is first sorted
in descending order of predicted relevance, the final merge
does not merely concatenate top-N pop and top-N tail. Instead,
we reference the user’s historical blueprint of head–tail
consumption to guide how these items are interleaved.
Suppose, for example, that positions in the user’s real rating
list alternately reflect head or tail preferences. Then for each
rank k in the top-N recommendation, we:
1) Determine whether the kth historical position is head or
tail in the user’s consumption blueprint.
2) If it is head, pick the next highest-scoring item from
Mpop’s descending list.
3) If it is tail, choose the next highest-scoring item from
Mtail’s descending list.
This process yields a smart top-N list that matches the
user’s empirically observed ratio of interest in head vs.
tail content without enforcing a uniform or arbitrary blend.
By anchoring the final merge to the user’s documented
head–tail trajectory, the system more faithfully reflects
individualized consumption patterns, thereby ensuring that
diverse, personalized recommendations emerge in a manner
consistent with the user’s actual historical preferences.
